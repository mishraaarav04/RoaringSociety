The Legacy of World War I

Four direct effects of World War I shaped the social conflicts of the 1920s.

    Gallery I

    The Great Migration and Racial Tensions

    Research question: What pushed Black Southerners north and pulled them toward Northern and Midwestern cities, and how did that movement intensify racial conflict?

    Push factors (leaving the South)

    • Economic: the boll weevil destroyed cotton crops, and tenant farming and sharecropping created debt traps.
    • Social and political: systemic disenfranchisement (poll taxes, literacy tests), Jim Crow segregation, and extreme racial violence, including lynchings.

    Pull factors (moving to the North and Midwest)

    • Economic: wartime labor shortages created high-paying factory jobs in Chicago, Detroit, New York, and Pittsburgh.
    • Social: greater voting rights, better access to public education, and growing urban African American communities.

    Racial tensions and mass violence

    • The Red Summer of 1919: more than 20 race riots erupted across the United States.
    • The Tulsa Race Massacre (1921): white mobs burned the Black business district of Greenwood, known as “Black Wall Street,” after unproven allegations against a young Black man. Up to 300 African Americans died, and the neighborhood was destroyed.

    The revived Ku Klux Klan

    • Re-founded at Stone Mountain, Georgia, in 1915, the 1920s Klan spread beyond the Reconstruction-era South into the Midwest and western cities, such as those in Indiana.
    • Its targets widened from Black Americans to Roman Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and modern or secular social trends.
    • Membership peaked at an estimated 4 to 5 million by the mid-1920s. The Klan used lobbying, rallies, and violence under the banner of “100% Americanism.”

    Content note: this gallery discusses racial violence. Images are limited to aftermath and text, and the museum lets you skip sensitive rooms.

    Gallery II

    The Birth of the ACLU

    Research question: How did wartime and postwar repression lead to organized civil-liberties advocacy in 1920?

    • Context. The ACLU formed in 1920 in response to wartime civil-liberties violations under the Espionage and Sedition Acts and the Palmer Raids of 1919–1920. Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer ordered warrantless mass arrests and deportations of suspected radicals without due process.
    • Founders. Key founders included Roger Baldwin, Crystal Eastman, and Jane Addams.
    • Mission. The ACLU defends constitutional rights, especially First Amendment freedoms of speech, assembly, and press and the right to due process, regardless of the political beliefs of the accused.
    • Early actions. In the 1920s it provided legal defense in high-profile cases such as the Scopes Trial (1925) and defended labor organizers and anti-war dissidents.
    Gallery III

    Intolerance, Xenophobia, and the First Red Scare

    Research question: How did fear of foreigners and radicals shape immigration restriction and anti-labor policy?

    The First Red Scare (1919–1920)

    It was fueled by the 1917 Bolshevik Revolution, postwar economic turmoil, and high-profile anarchist bombings.

    The Sacco and Vanzetti trial (1921–1927)

    Italian immigrants Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, self-proclaimed anarchists, were convicted of a robbery and murder in Massachusetts. Despite weak physical evidence and unreliable witnesses, both were executed in 1927. The case exposed judicial bias, nativism, and fear of radical ideologies.

    Immigration restriction

    Suppression of labor unions

    • Strikes such as the 1919 Boston Police Strike and the Seattle General Strike were portrayed in the media as communist plots.
    • Business groups promoted the “American Plan,” open-shop policies that suppressed union membership. Union enrollment fell sharply over the decade.
    Gallery IV

    Gitlow v. New York (1925)

    Research question: Why does Gitlow matter in the history of free-speech protection?

    • Background. Benjamin Gitlow, a socialist leader, was convicted under New York’s Criminal Anarchy Law for publishing the Left Wing Manifesto, which advocated overthrowing the government.
    • Decision. The Supreme Court upheld the conviction, ruling that speech encouraging violent revolution threatens public safety.
    • Significance. Even so, the Court established that the First Amendment’s protection of free speech applies to state governments, not just the federal government, through the Due Process Clause of the 14th Amendment.
    • Incorporation. This began the selective incorporation doctrine, which expanded federal protection of individual constitutional rights against state interference.
    Gallery V

    Prohibition

    Research question: How did the intended effects of the 18th Amendment and the Volstead Act differ from their actual consequences?

    Intended effects. Supporters aimed to eliminate alcohol abuse, reduce domestic violence, improve workplace productivity, lower crime, and close saloons.

    Unintended effects

    • Organized crime. Bootlegging empires flourished, such as Al Capone’s in Chicago, earning billions of dollars through illegal alcohol distribution and racketeering.
    • Speakeasies and an underground economy. Illicit drinking establishments thrived in an unregulated alcohol trade.
    • Erosion of respect for law. Millions of law-abiding citizens routinely broke the law, and corruption spread through police departments, courts, and federal Prohibition agents.
    • Economic loss. Governments lost millions in alcohol tax revenue while enforcement spending rose.
    • Repeal. The 21st Amendment ended Prohibition in 1933.

        Museum Design Plan

        Core representations

        The museum includes at least five distinct artifacts or media items, for example:

        • A primary-document excerpt from Gitlow v. New York
        • A photograph or map of Great Migration routes
        • Audio clips or primary quotations from Sacco and Vanzetti
        • Photographs and newspaper clippings of Prohibition raids
        • Photographs of Klan marches in Washington, D.C.

        Self-guided navigation

        The platform provides clear menus and visual tools so visitors can explore independently and always find their way back to the main museum: a minimap, a full map, a teleport menu, and a location banner.

        Audio and video

        Narrated voiceovers, primary-source audio readings, and a newsreel viewer support the galleries, all with captions.

        Research Room

        A formatted bibliography, with primary and secondary sources separated, anchors the project’s research.

            The Roaring Society

            Explore how freedom and civil liberties were contested in 1920s America. Here is how to complete your visit:

            1. Start at the Visitor Directory. Walk to the directory in the Entrance Hall, look at it and press E.
            2. Follow Galleries I–V. Begin in Gallery I in the southwest wing, then continue around the courtyard. Press T to teleport or M for the map.
            3. Study the exhibits. Examine gold primary sources, silver secondary sources and green evidence plaques with E.
            4. Complete quests. Press Q for tasks and exact locations. Pin a quest to mark its destination. Read sources and finish the activities to earn points and five gallery stamps.
            5. Build your final argument. Visit the Research Room in the east wing, then answer the two Guiding Questions in the Synthesis Gallery in the southeast wing.
